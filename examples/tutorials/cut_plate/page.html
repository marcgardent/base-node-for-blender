Cut plate

A brass nameplate: pointed ends, round corners, two screw holes, a sunken panel of blue enamel with the letters standing in it. Every shape on it is a closed line, drawn in 2D, and one rule turns the pile of lines into faces: a point is metal when an odd number of lines surround it. No boolean, no modelling by hand, and only the free standard library: fillet2d for the corners, planar2d for the faces.

Before you start

You need Blender 5 and the Base Node add-on. The modules fillet2d and planar2d come with it, in its free standard library: a file that says use planar2d finds it there.

Every block below is a complete .bn file, its code beside the nodes it builds. Two ways to try a step:

  • Drag it. Drag the file icon of its block, or its title, onto empty space in Blender's 3D Viewport, and keep Instantiate the Example: a new object carries cut_plate at the 3D cursor. A click on the file icon downloads the file instead.
  • Paste it. Press the copy button of its block, open a Geometry Node editor, press Ctrl+Shift+V and pick Add as Modifier, on any mesh object: the graph draws its own lines.

The pictures are rendered from these exact files by render.py, which also measures each step's plate and checks the numbers this page gives. Sizes are in Blender units: the plate is 4.4 long. Scale the object to the size you want.

1. A closed line, its face, a plate

The plate starts as its outline: a closed line in the XY plane. A face is what is inside it; a plate is that face, given a thickness. Keeping the shape as lines until the end is what makes the next steps cheap: rounding a corner, adding a hole or a letter is adding or changing a line, never editing a mesh.

{{code step1_outline.bn cut_plate outline layer}}
  • The outline. CurveCircle with a resolution of 6 is a hexagon: six points on a circle of radius 1, the first on the x axis. TransformGeometry stretches it: the tips go to x = ±2.2, half the length. The four other points are at 60°, where y is sin 60° = 0.866 = 1.732 / 2, so dividing width by 1.732 puts the long edges at y = ±0.65, half the width.
  • Its face. planar_faces of planar2d fills closed lines with FillCurve. One closed line, one face: here a hexagon cut into triangles. It first welds lines that meet end to end, within its tolerance, so three separate lines meeting at their ends fill as a triangle. Every shape of this plate is a closed line already.
  • A thickness. layer lifts the face to bottom, then ExtrudeMesh pushes it up to top: its offset is the direction, straight up, (0.0, 0.0, 1.0), and its offset_scale the distance, top - bottom; without an offset, each face would go along its normal. The face becomes the top, and walls join it to where it was. Extruding leaves nothing underneath, so cut_plate adds the face again, turned over by FlipFaces so that it faces down: the underside.

2. Round corners, a radius per corner

Sharp corners look unfinished on metal, and catch. fillet2d's fillet_polygon rounds them: it replaces each corner by an arc of the radius it is given, tangent to both sides.

{{code step2_corners.bn rounded corner_radius cut_plate}}
  • A radius per corner. The radius is a field: it is read at each point of the curve. corner_radius gives 0.3 to the two tips, the points on the x axis, and 0.1 to the four others. Large arcs make the ends soft, small ones keep the long edges straight.
  • Its points. fillet_polygon draws each arc as segments straight segments, here 12: the outline stays a poly curve, its corners replaced by points along the arcs. Fewer segments show facets on the round tips; more make the arcs smoother and the mesh heavier.

The tips are corners of 61.2°. The centre of an arc of radius 0.3 tangent to both sides is 0.59 in from the tip, at x = 1.61, so the rounded tip now ends at x = 1.91 instead of 2.2.

Try: set segments to 1 in rounded: every corner is cut by one straight segment, a chamfer. Step 5 cuts its panel's corners with chamfer_polygon, made for that.

3. Two screw holes, centred on the round tips

A screw hole in a rounded tip looks right only when it is centred on the arc: the ring of metal around it is then as wide all round. That centre is not on the outline, and the curve does not store it. fillet2d's fillet_corner computes it from three points: the corner and its two neighbours.

{{code step3_holes.bn corner_centres screw_holes cut_plate}}
  1. The neighbours. SampleIndex reads the position of the points before and after each point, at i − 1 and i + 1. The outline is closed: mod(…, n) wraps the indices, so the point before point 0 is point 5.
  2. The centre. fillet_corner takes the directions to the two neighbours and the angle between them. The centre of a circle of radius r tangent to both sides is on the bisector, at r / sin(angle / 2) from the corner: at the tips, 0.3 / sin 30.6° = 0.59.
  3. On points. CurveToPoints[EVALUATED] of a poly curve gives its own points, in their order, so Index() there is the index of the corner on the curve. SetPosition moves each point to its arc's centre.
  4. The holes. screw_holes keeps the two centres on the x axis, at x = ±1.61, and puts a circle of radius 0.1 on each. It asks corner_radius for the radius, the same function the fillet used: change the tips' radius there, and the holes follow.

Now the rule that does all the cutting. FillCurve fills by even-odd: from any point, count the lines around it. One line, the edge: inside, metal. Two lines, the edge and a hole: outside again, nothing. So the holes are cut by giving planar_faces their circles together with the outline, rounded(polygon) + screw_holes(polygon). Each closed line added flips what it surrounds.

4. Letters cut through: a stencil

Letters are closed lines too, so even-odd cuts them like the holes. That makes a stencil, and shows the stencil's oldest problem.

{{code step4_stencil.bn lettering cut_plate}}

lettering turns a text into curves: StringToCurves gives a curve instance per letter, RealizeInstances makes them plain curves. A letter is one closed line, or more: B is its outside and two counters, the closed holes of the letter. Counted from the plate, a letter's inside is surrounded by two lines, so it is cut out. Inside a counter there are three: metal again, a piece of plate in the middle of a hole, attached to nothing.

planar2d's shape_builder_pick makes the cut real. It fills the lines as planar_faces does, groups its faces into even-odd regions, faces joined by an edge, and keeps only the regions where a seed point falls, or the nearest one within pick_radius. One seed at (0, 0.6), just inside the top edge at 0.65, keeps the plate; every piece that would fall out of a real stencil goes. render.py counts them: 5, two in the B and one in each of A, O and D.

This is why stencil letters have bridges, gaps that tie the counters to the plate. Here we take the other way out: stop cutting through.

5. A sunken panel, raised letters, by parity

A nameplate keeps its letters by not cutting through: the panel around them is sunk into the plate, and the letters stand at the plate's height. Even-odd tells, for each point, whether it is high or low, if the panel is one more closed line.

{{code step5_engraved.bn panel cut_plate}}

panel is a rectangle 2.6 by 0.62 with its corners cut, by chamfer_polygon on the curve of Quadrilateral. Its distance is how far from the corner the cut starts, along each side: 0.1 here, whatever the corner's angle.

WhereLines around itCountOn the plate
The plate, outside the panelthe edge1, oddhigh
A screw holethe edge, the hole2, evennothing
The panel, between the lettersthe edge, the panel2, evenlow
A letterthe edge, the panel, the letter3, oddhigh
A counterthe edge, the panel, the letter, the counter4, evenlow

So the plate is two layers. under, the edge and the holes, goes from 0 to 0.055, thickness − depth, with its underside. raised, everything odd, goes from 0.055 to 0.08 on top of it. Where raised has no face, the top of under shows, 0.025 lower: that is the panel's floor. The counters are part of that floor now, held by the whole plate beneath them. The plate is 11 separate pieces: under, its underside, the raised frame and 8 raised letters, B, A, S, E, N, O, D, E.

6. Brass and enamel

An enamelled nameplate is made just as step 5 is: the metal is cast or etched with the panel sunk, then the panel is filled with coloured glass, which is fired and polished. The geometry needs one more layer, the enamel, and two materials.

{{code step6_enamel.bn cut_plate Brass Enamel}}
  • The enamel's faces. ground is planar_faces of the panel and the letters, without the edge: inside the panel 1 line, filled; inside a letter 2, empty; inside a counter 3, filled. It is exactly the panel's floor of step 5.
  • Its layer. From the floor, 0.055, up to fill of the depth: at 0.6, to 0.07, 0.01 below the brass. The letters stand out of the enamel by that much, and their walls catch the light. The plate is now 17 pieces: step 5's 11, the panel's enamel and 5 counters filled.
  • Two materials. SetMaterial gives Brass to the metal and Enamel to the enamel before JoinGeometry puts them together: each face keeps its own.
  • Brass is a metal, metallic: 1.0: its colour is the colour of its reflections, a warm yellow. Brushed metal is rough in fine lines: p * (1.0, 100.0, 1.0) squeezes the noise a hundred times across y, so it changes fast across the plate and slowly along it, and the roughness goes from 0.18 to 0.34 in streaks along x. TextureCoordinate().object pins the noise to the object: no UV map is needed.
  • Enamel is a dark blue at roughness 0.3, under a clear coat, coat_weight: 1.0, as smooth as glass, coat_roughness: 0.02: the colour is satin, the shine on top of it sharp.

Try: set text in lettering to your own name. Set fill to 1.0: the enamel comes up level with the brass, as on a polished badge.

The table is a dark matt square, the light two area lights: a large one behind the plate, where its top mirrors it to the camera, and one in front on the left. The render is Cycles, 64 samples, denoised.
